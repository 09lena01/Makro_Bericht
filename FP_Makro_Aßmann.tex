	\chapter{Introduction} \label{chapter:introduction}
	\section{Lipoic Acid}
	\subsection{General Background}
	\Gls{LA} is a cyclic dithiol which is naturally occurring in both procaryotes and eukaryotes, and is displayed in \autoref{fig:LA_structure}. \Gls{LA} has antioxidant properties and is a cofactor in the citric acid cycle. Its chirality provides two enantiomers wherein the (\textit{R})-(+)-\gls{LA} is biologically active. The experimental data show a S-S bond length of \SI{205,3}{\pico\meter}. It has lipophilic properties and can undergo \gls{ROP} which is why it has been selected as a monomer for this work.\cite{smith_lipoic_2004,moura_lipoic_2015,rusu_vibrational_2022}
	\begin{figure}[H]
		\centering
		\includegraphics[width=0.4\textwidth]{Graphs/Lipoic_Acid.png}
		\caption{Chemical Structure of racemic \glsentryfull{LA}.}
		\label{fig:LA_structure}
	\end{figure}\noindent
	The \gls{ROP} for \gls{LA} can be anionic, cationic or radical which is why storing \gls{LA} under UV protection and in the fridge is essential to prevent unwanted polymerization. Additionally, the carboxylic acid can be functionalized to obtain hyperbranched polymers.\cite{liu_architecture-controlled_2019,koelbl_greener_2025,tang_lipoates_2015,debnath_dual-degradable_2025,albanese_controlled-radical_2023}
	\subsection{Esterification}
	To prevent ionization of the carboxylic acid and therefore unwanted polymerization, esterification is a method to modify the molecule in a minor way to retain most of its chemical properties for \gls{ROP}, but without the carboxylic acid. With \gls{LA}, the chosen mechanisms are either Fischer or Steglich esterification.\\
	The Fischer esterification is an acid catalyzed esterification under reflux. As a solvent, the alcohol is often used in excess. Here, the carbonyl group is activated by the catalyst and attacked by the nucleophilic alcohol. Water is eliminated after the alcohol addition which results in the ester.\cite{mannu_fischer-speier_2024}\\
	Alternatively, the Steglich esterification operates with a carbodiimide as a coupling agent at low temperatures between \SIrange{0}{20}{\degreeCelsius} and often \gls{DMAP} as an organocatalyst. Traditionally, \gls{DCM}, \gls{DMF}, \gls{THF} or acetonitrile are used as a solvent.\cite{munawar_steglich_2024,jordan_solvent-reagent_2021}\\
	For \gls{LA}, most esters for \gls{ROP} are the methyl or ethyl lipoate. The Steglich esterification has been the more reliable choice or this monomer, but most recently the Fischer esterification has been adapted for \gls{LA} for a more green synthesis.\cite{koelbl_greener_2025}
	\begin{figure}[H]
		\centering
		\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{Graphs/LpAlk_monomer_synthesis.png}
		\caption{Overview of Steglich and Fischer esterification of \glsxtrfull{LA}.}
		\label{fig:esterification_overview}
	\end{figure}
	\subsection{Degradation of Poly(Lipoate)s}
	The polymers of lipoic acid derivatives that can be obtained via \gls{ROP} are connected via disulfide bonds which makes them susceptible to depolymerization by reaction with reducing agents. These commonly chosen reducing agents for poly(lipoate)s are \gls{DTT} or \gls{TCEP} and result in the cleavage of the disulfides and obtaining the dithiol. The \gls{DTT} can be used to mimic intracellular and therefore aqueous conditions. \gls{TCEP} on the other hand is used in organic solvents. \cite{albanese_controlled-radical_2023,debnath_dual-degradable_2025,yang_radical_2026}
	\section{\glsentrytitlecase{RAFT}{long} Polymerization}
	\subsection{General Background}
	Radical polymerizations can have different approaches, with the \gls{FRP} as the most basic option, which results in high \gls{dispersity} due to recomination or disproportionation during the termination step. On the other hand, \gls{RDRP} takes a different approach. With names, which are discouraged by the \gls{IUPAC}, like "controlled" radical polymerization or pseudo-living polymerization the nature of these mechanisms become clear: the \glsxtrlong{dispersity} is in a narrow range as it is in real living polymerizations, such as anionic or cationic ones. Most \gls{RDRP} happen with an equilibrium which often results in dormant states, so that chain extensions become possible.\cite{shipp_reversible-deactivation_2011,jenkins_terminology_2009}\\
	One example for \gls{RDRP} reactions is \gls{RAFT} polymerization. It operates with a \gls{CTA} which has a stabilizing group to maintain equilibria. The initiation can be through chemical, thermal or photo initiation, depending on the \gls{RAFT} agent and the schematic \gls{RAFT} mechanism is depicted in \autoref{fig:RAFT_mechanism}.\cite{moad_fundamentals_2021}
	\begin{figure}[H]
		\centering
		\includegraphics[width=.8\textwidth]{Graphs/RAFT_Mechanism.jpg}
		\caption[Visualization of addition and fragmentation of the \gls{RAFT} mechanism.]{Visualization of addition and fragmentation of the \gls{RAFT} mechanism.\cite{moad_fundamentals_2021}}
		\label{fig:RAFT_mechanism}
	\end{figure}\noindent
	The general structure of the \gls{CTA} is depicted in \autoref{fig:RAFT_agent}. The basic structure is composed of a thiocarbonylthio group with varying stabilizing ("Z") and leaving ("R") groups. The kinetics for the addition of propagating radicals and the fragmentaion of intermediate radicals can be altered by the stabilizing group, whereas the leaving group is responsible for the partition coefficient and needs to be able to reinitiate the polymerization. The partition coefficient gives insight on intermediate radicals preferring to return to starting materials or fragment to products.\cite{keddie_raft_2012}
	\begin{figure}[H]
		\centering
		\includegraphics[width=.4\textwidth]{Graphs/RAFT_agents_structure.png}
		\caption{General structure of \gls{RAFT} agents.}
		\label{fig:RAFT_agent}
	\end{figure}\noindent
	A wide variety of Z- and R-groups have been tested in \gls{RAFT} agents for commonly used monomers. \autoref{fig:RAFT_ZR} shows the compatibility for these monomers and can be used as a guide to select the correct \gls{CTA}. Due to the nature of \gls{RDRP}, chain extensions for copolymers can be performed, but the compatibility of the \gls{RAFT} agents with the different monomers needs to be accounted for.
	\begin{figure}[H]
		\centering
		\includegraphics[width=.9\textwidth]{Graphs/RAFT_agents.png}
		\caption[Stabilizing and leaving groups for \gls{RAFT} agents and their compatibility with monomers.]{Different stabilizing and leaving groups for \gls{RAFT} agents and their compatibility with common monomers.\cite{noauthor_lets_nodate}}
		\label{fig:RAFT_ZR}
	\end{figure}
	\subsection{Application to Lipoates}
	The suitability of leaving and stabilizing groups from \autoref{fig:RAFT_ZR} is for common monomers and for similar structures estimates based on the trend can be made. As dithiols are not part of the more default monomers, it is difficult to predict their reactivity with \gls{CTA}. Finding suitable \gls{RAFT} agents has been part of more recent research and is an ongoing process. For homopolymers, the work by K. \citeauthor{lee_controlled_2025} showed a small variety of tested \gls{CTA} for photo-initiated \gls{RAFT} polymerization of lipoates. D. \citeauthor{lee_light-mediated_2026}'s work tested three different \gls{RAFT} agents for copolymerization of \gls{LA} via photo-initiation. \cite{lee_light-mediated_2026,lee_controlled_2025}
	\section{\glsentrytitlecase{PISA}{long}}
	\subsection{General Background}
	\Gls{PISA} describes the assembly of amphiphilic block copolymers upon polymerization in solvent, which usually is water. The assembly of the block copolymers then result in the formation of nano-structures which can occur in different morphologies, depending on the lengths of the different blocks amongst other factors. The procedure is shown in \autoref{fig:PISA}: a hydrophilic polymer with a "living" endgroup -- an endgroup that allows chain extension and is compatible with the second monomer-- is in solution with a monomer which is either hydrophobic for which emulsion polymerization is needed, or a monomer with a corresponding hydrophobic polymer which would result in dispersion polymerization. As the chain chain grows, the amphiphilic character of the block copolymer increases until it eventually self-assembles into a particle. \cite{charleux_polymerization-induced_2012,liu_polymerization_2020,doncom_dispersity_2017}
	\begin{figure}[H]
		\centering
		\includegraphics[width=.55\textwidth]{Graphs/PISA_scheme.png}
		\caption[Schematic depiction of the reaction steps for \glsxtrfull{PISA}.]{Schematic depiction of the reaction steps for \glsxtrfull{PISA}. \cite{charleux_polymerization-induced_2012}}
		\label{fig:PISA}
	\end{figure}
	\subsection{Reverse Sequence \glsentrytitlecase{PISA}{long}}
	Recent developments show a different approach for \gls{PISA}. Here, a hydrophobic polymer with a stabilizing endgroup for aqueous conditions is synthesized as the first step. This results in latex particles. By adding a second, hydrophilic monomer, chain extension can be possible which leads to amphiphilic particles. \cite{ali_synthesis_2007,buksa_synthesis_2023}

	\subsection{\glsentrytitlecase{SEC}{long}}
	\Gls{SEC} is an analysis method to characterize polymers in regard to their molecular weight. This provides information on the molar mass distribution and characteristic values, such as the \gls{Mn}, \gls{Mw} and the \gls{dispersity}.\\
	The measurements for the \gls{SEC} analysis were performed on the instruments within the Chair of Macromolecular Chemistry at \gls{HHU} according to the established procedure within the group. The majority of the measurements have been collected on the instrument with \gls{DMF} as a solvent, the remaining measurements have been made on the \gls{THF} instrument. The data has been exported as an ASCII file and plotted on OriginPro 2025 v10.2.
	\subsection{\glsentrytitlecase{NMR}{long}}
	The technique \gls{NMR} spectroscopy can be used to determine the chemical composition, and by that also the purity, of chemicals, to obtain the conversion of a and monitor the polymerization reaction.\\
	For the analysis, \textsuperscript{1}\textit{H}-\gls{NMR} measurements were performed by the \gls{CEMSA} on the \SI{600}{\mega\hertz} spectrometer. The results have been displayed and analyzed via the software MestReNova v17.0.1 which has been obtained through the chemistry department at Heinrich Heine University.\newpage
	\subsubsection{\Glsentrylong{DOSY}}
	\Gls{DOSY} is a form of \gls{NMR} spectroscopy wherein the different signals are measured as a function of their chemical shift as well as their \gls{Diffusion}. This allows the differentiation between different molecules within a mixture which makes it a powerful analysis for analyzing block copolymers. Within the \gls{CEMSA}, the measurments have been performed on the \SI{600}{\mega\hertz} Evo Neo spectrometer. For the analysis, the software TopSpin 5.0.0 Processing by Bruker has been used. The software can be acquired through the academic processing license on the Bruker website.\cite{noauthor_topspin_nodate}\\
	The analysis of \gls{DOSY} spectra can be done the following way:
	\begin{enumerate}
		\item Make a copy of the folder with the raw data
		\item Navigate to "\textit{/pdata/1}" within the copied raw data folder and localize the file called "\textit{2rr}"
		\item Open TopSpin
		\item Drag and drop the file "\textit{2rr}" into TopSpin
		\item Enter the following commands into the command line after the previous one is done:
		\begin{enumerate}
			\item xf2
			\item abs2
			\item eddosy
			\item setdiffparm
			\item dosy2d
		\end{enumerate}
		\item TopSpin can be closed once the commands have been run and MestReNova can be opened
		\item The edited folder with the data can be dragged and dropped into MestReNova
		\item In the \textit{Stacked} tab, the button \textit{DOSY Plot} needs to be pressed
		\item The display of the spectra can be edited via the \textit{Properties} window as usual
	\end{enumerate}\newpage
	\subsection{\glsentrytitlecase{MALDI}{long}}
	In order to receive information on the end groups of polymers, \gls{MALDI} can be measured by the \gls{CEMSA}. For this, the samples need to be in solution and an approximate molar mass is needed to set the measurement window. The concentration should be approximately \SI{1}{\milli\gram\per\milli\liter} and for the samples within this research work, \gls{THF} has been selected as a solvent.\\
	The analysis of the \gls{MALDI} spectra can be done as follows:

				\item The peak list can be edited in the tab \textit{Mass Liss}. \\

	\subsection{\glsentrytitlecase{DLS}{long}}
	\Gls{DLS} is a method for determining the \gls{rh} of nano- and microparticles in solution. The measurements can be done on the instrument within the Chair of Physical Chemistry I and according to the instructions that can be provided by the Chair of Marcomolecular Chemistry at \gls{HHU}. Adding to said instruction, it is important to export the data separated by a tab instead if a comma.\\
	For this work, the raw data and the size distribution have been exported. The raw data entails some of the measurement parameters, such as the \gls{eta}, \gls{T}, the number of runs and their duration. Additionally, the \gls{rh} of the species within the sample and the \gls{PDI} can be found. The size distribution data is necessary for plotting the size distribution. For this, the data of a single run, which is displayed in one row, is copied into Microsoft Excel. The second half of that row entails the intensity data points. These are cut and pasted below the radii. The edited data can then be paste transposed and plotted in OriginPro 2025 (v10.2).
